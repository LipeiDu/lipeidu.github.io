## Longitudinal and baryon dynamics of QCD matter

At lower collision energies, boost invariance breaks down and the net-baryon density of the produced matter becomes increasingly important. Understanding this regime requires a fully three-dimensional description of how energy and conserved baryon charge are deposited, transported, and redistributed during the collision. My work in this direction has developed from building dynamical descriptions of baryon-rich matter to using rapidity-dependent observables to constrain the collision dynamics and reconstruct the thermodynamic properties of the produced medium.

**Dynamical modeling of baryon-rich matter.** My early work focused on developing the theoretical tools needed to describe heavy-ion collisions at finite net-baryon density. This included dynamical initialization schemes and a full (3+1)-dimensional viscous hydrodynamic framework incorporating the transport of conserved baryon charge. These developments made it possible to follow baryon-rich QCD matter without assuming boost invariance and to study how longitudinal structure, baryon transport, and hydrodynamic expansion jointly shape final-state observables.

**Baryon stopping and the equation of state.** I subsequently used rapidity-dependent observables to investigate how the initial longitudinal structure is encoded in measurable final states. In particular, identified-particle directed flow provides complementary information on how incoming baryon charge is stopped and distributed, and on the pressure response of finite-density QCD matter. This turns rapidity-dependent flow from a quantity to be reproduced into a diagnostic of the initial state and the equation of state.

**Mapping QCD matter across rapidity.** Building on this framework, I carried out systematic rapidity scans over Beam Energy Scan collision energies. These studies showed that a collision cannot in general be represented by a single thermodynamic point: different rapidity regions sample different temperatures, baryon chemical potentials, and dynamical histories. By calibrating realistic (3+1)-dimensional simulations to rapidity-dependent data, I studied the energy and baryon charge deposited into the fireball and reconstructed the longitudinal thermodynamic structure of baryon-rich QCD matter.

**Connecting hydrodynamic and microscopic descriptions.** More recently, I have also developed methods for extracting local thermodynamic properties directly from microscopic transport simulations by reconstructing the energy-momentum tensor and conserved-charge currents. Together, these studies progress from building the dynamical framework, to constraining baryon stopping and the equation of state, to using rapidity as a differential probe of the regions of the QCD phase diagram explored in heavy-ion collisions.

## Critical fluctuations and the search for the QCD critical point

A major goal of the RHIC Beam Energy Scan is to search for the QCD critical point through enhanced fluctuations and correlations of conserved charges. Connecting such signatures to the underlying critical physics is challenging because the matter created in heavy-ion collisions is finite and rapidly evolving, while measured fluctuations are also affected by global conservation, finite acceptance, and longitudinal inhomogeneity. My research has followed this chain of problems, from the dynamics of critical fluctuations to experimentally accessible observables designed to distinguish critical physics from noncritical backgrounds.

**Nonequilibrium critical dynamics.** My early work focused on how critical fluctuations evolve in an expanding medium. Using dynamical descriptions of slow critical modes, I studied the interplay of critical slowing down, expansion, advection, and relaxation near the QCD critical point. I subsequently investigated the coupling between critical dynamics and baryon transport, including how critical behavior affects baryon diffusion and the evolution of baryon-rich matter through the QCD phase diagram.

**From critical dynamics to realistic observables.** More recently, my work has increasingly focused on the experimental interpretation of conserved-charge fluctuations. We studied rapidity-dependent net-baryon cumulants including global baryon-number conservation, finite acceptance, and longitudinal inhomogeneity, providing realistic noncritical baselines for fluctuation measurements. Recent calculations have also incorporated critical fluctuations into hydrodynamic predictions for experimentally measured net-proton cumulants.

**Designing cleaner critical signatures.** A further direction is to develop observables in which critical and noncritical physics organize themselves differently. In cross-rapidity net-baryon cumulants, global baryon-number conservation produces a negative correlation between separated rapidity windows, whereas a common long-wavelength critical mode produces a positive one. This qualitative difference makes it possible to estimate and subtract the leading conservation contribution and construct observables with enhanced sensitivity to long-range critical correlations.

Together, these studies form a progression from nonequilibrium critical dynamics, through realistic fluctuation baselines, to the design of cleaner experimental signatures of QCD criticality.

## Electromagnetic probes and multimessenger perspectives

Photons and dileptons are emitted throughout the evolution of a heavy-ion collision and escape the strongly interacting medium with little final-state interaction. They therefore provide direct access to stages that are difficult to reconstruct from hadronic observables alone. At the same time, electromagnetic radiation integrates over matter with changing temperature, collective flow, composition, and degree of equilibration, making the extraction of quantitative medium properties a nontrivial inverse problem.

My work in this direction has developed from realistic calculations of dilepton radiation to the use of complementary electromagnetic probes for extracting the early temperature and collective expansion of QCD matter.

**Dilepton thermometry and equilibration.** An important focus of my work has been intermediate-mass dileptons, whose invariant-mass spectra provide a comparatively clean window into the thermal history of the QGP. Using next-to-leading-order thermal dilepton rates, including finite baryon chemical potential, we studied dilepton production in realistic (3+1)-dimensional simulations and demonstrated quantitatively that the inverse slope of the intermediate-mass spectrum closely tracks the early QGP temperature. I have also studied how dilepton yields and anisotropic flow respond to thermal and chemical equilibration and to different early-time sources.

**Multimessenger electromagnetic tomography.** A single electromagnetic spectrum generally reflects several medium properties at once. Dilepton invariant-mass spectra provide a comparatively flow-insensitive measure of the thermal scale, while photon transverse-momentum spectra are strongly blue-shifted by collective expansion. Their complementary sensitivities therefore provide a way to disentangle temperature and radial flow. Building on this idea, I developed a photon–dilepton method in which the dilepton-inferred temperature determines the photon spectral slope expected without transverse flow, providing the reference needed to isolate the flow-induced blue shift and extract an effective radial flow of the QGP before freeze-out.

**Physical origin and limits.** More recently, I have focused on understanding why these extraction methods work, what physical quantities they measure, and under what conditions their interpretation remains reliable. For dilepton thermometry, the inverse slope is closely related to an emission-weighted harmonic mean of the temperatures sampled by the radiation. For photon–dilepton tomography, the extracted blue shift is connected to the flow velocity sampled by photon emission. These studies also identify changes in the cooling history and early electromagnetic source composition as important sources of nonuniversality, and show how multiple mass and momentum windows can provide additional diagnostic information.

This progression—from dilepton thermometry, to multimessenger radial-flow tomography, to understanding the physical meaning and limitations of these observables—forms a continuing effort to develop electromagnetic radiation into a quantitative diagnostic toolkit for the early-time properties and dynamics of QCD matter.

## Collective fluctuations and dynamical correlations across momentum scales

Average spectra and flow observables characterize the mean evolution of the QGP, while event-by-event fluctuations provide additional information on how variations in the initial state and subsequent dynamics propagate through the collision. My work in this direction uses transverse-momentum fluctuations to resolve the structure of collective dynamics and, more recently, to explore dynamical connections between the soft, hadronization, and hard sectors of heavy-ion collisions.

**Mean-$p_T$ fluctuations and collective dynamics.** I studied event-by-event mean-$p_T$ fluctuations using a Bayesian-calibrated multistage hydrodynamic framework, with particular attention to separating genuine dynamical effects from those induced by kinematic acceptance and observable definitions. These studies show that part of the apparent beam-energy dependence arises from kinematic projection effects, while the remaining structure provides an independent test of the collective dynamics encoded in calibrated hydrodynamic descriptions.

**Momentum-differential radial-flow fluctuations.** To resolve these fluctuations in momentum, I investigated the $p_T$-differential radial-flow observable $v_0(p_T)$ across top RHIC and LHC energies. Its shape responds differently to transport coefficients, particlization, hadronic rescattering, and initial-state granularity. At low $p_T$, a combined rescaling reveals an approximate universality across collision energies and model variations, separating robust collective behavior from more model-dependent structures that emerge at higher momentum.

**Dynamical correlations across momentum scales.** More recently, I extended this fluctuation framework beyond the soft sector to ask how dynamical coherence evolves as particle production changes from collective flow through hadronization to hard processes. Using a reference-aligned covariance matrix and its fluctuation eigenmodes, we find a progression from an approximately single coherent soft mode to multi-mode dynamics involving coalescence and jet quenching. This provides experimentally accessible measures of how strongly different momentum sectors remain dynamically connected, offering a unified way to study correlations across traditionally separated regimes of QGP physics.

Together, these studies progress from global transverse-momentum fluctuations, to their momentum-differential structure, to the dynamical correlations that connect soft collective physics with hadronization and hard probes.
